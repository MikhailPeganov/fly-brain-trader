Fly-Brain Trader Blind Test

A Drosophila-inspired network that feels "pain" when a trade loses and "happiness" when it wins was pre-trained on 40 stocks, then traded 20 stocks and a trading day it had never seen. Four statistical tests asked whether its results reflect a real strategy or luck.

Verdict
No evidence of a real strategy. Its results are consistent with luck.

On the pre-registered primary test (20 hidden stocks, Jan 2024 to Oct 2026), shuffling the fly's own trades in time produced a Sharpe ratio at least as good of the time. We cannot reject "it is just noise" at the 5% level.

What the model was not given

Two kinds of hidden information

Stocks were split at random before anything was trained. The fly learned only from the 40 training stocks, and its settings were chosen on 2022–2023 data from those same stocks. The settings were locked in code before any test data was read.

All tests

Every test, in one place

One-sided tests of "the fly makes money / has information". The Newey–West t-test corrects for autocorrelation; the bootstrap resamples blocks of days; the alpha regression strips out market exposure; the hit-rate test compares against the hit rate a random trader with the same long/short mix would get.

Can the test be trusted?

The test catches real skill and ignores pure noise

Placebo: 40 markets with no signal

The whole pipeline rerun on prices whose daily returns had random signs, so nothing is predictable. of runs came out "significant", matching the 5% you expect by chance.

How the brain works

Mushroom body, rewired for markets

  • Senses: 16 factors from published quant research (short-term reversal, 12-1 momentum, 52-week high, low-volatility, abnormal volume, overnight gap and others) act as odours. Each is encoded by 5 projection neurons with different tuning, 80 in total.
  • Kenyon cells: 2,000 cells, each wired to 7 random projection neurons, matching fly connectome statistics. One inhibitory neuron (APL) keeps only the top 5% active.
  • Output neurons: a "go long" and a "go short" neuron read the Kenyon cells. Their imbalance sets the position size.
  • Feelings: after each trade, reward dopamine (happiness) or punishment dopamine (pain, 2× for losses) strengthens or weakens the synapses of the Kenyon cells that were active. A weaker "what if" signal teaches the side it did not take, and a run of pain makes it more cautious.

Limits of this test

  • The first live-day run looked brilliant (p = 0.001). That was a look-ahead bug: one feature used the day's own closing price. After the fix the edge vanished. Any result that looks this good deserves the same suspicion.
  • "Live" means a replay of real, unseen data. Markets are closed today, and no real orders were placed.
  • The universe is today's large caps, which flatters buy-and-hold (survivorship bias). It does not flatter the fly, which was market-neutral.
  • The pain and happiness are reward signals that shape learning. The model does not experience anything.
  • Settings were picked from 9 combinations on validation data, and every one of them lost money there, an early hint of no edge.
