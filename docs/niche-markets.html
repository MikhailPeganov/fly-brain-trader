Fly-Brain Niche Markets Test

Two long-only strategies built for the pain/happiness model were tested on four under-followed markets, with realistic costs. Settings were locked on 2022–23 data, then the fly traded 2024–26 blind. Every result is judged against the market and against a random picker following identical rules.

Verdict
No strategy is viable in any market. One hint of real skill, in Tokyo, doesn't survive correction for testing eight combinations.

The Tokyo stock picker beat the market by a year after costs, and beat random picking (p = ). Across 8 tests, that p-value becomes after correction. It deserves a live paper-trading test, not money.

The two strategies

Built for slow, costly markets

A · Slow picker

Every week the fly scores every tradeable stock and holds its top 20. It only sells a holding once it drops out of the top 60, so typical holds last months. Happiness/pain is each holding's weekly return relative to the market, minus the costs it caused.

B · Shock rebound

When a stock crashes on its own (a move worse than 2.5 standard deviations below its market), the fly decides whether to buy at the next open and hold for 10 days. Shocks it skips still teach it, through weaker "what if" dopamine.

Both are long-only because short-selling is banned or impractical in these markets. Costs per trade are half the live quoted spread, plus the price impact of a $10,000 order, plus a 0.10% commission. Vietnam also charges a 0.1% tax on sales. Only stocks trading at least $100,000 a day are tradeable.

Results · 8 pre-registered tests

Only the Tokyo picker came out ahead

Viability: did it beat an equal-weight portfolio of all tradeable stocks after costs? (one-sided Newey–West t-test). Model edge: did the fly beat a random picker that made the same number of trades on the same dates? (permutation test, 2,000–5,000 random runs). Corrected p-values use the Holm method across the 8 tests in each column.

What the fly learned to hold

Average factor score of its holdings minus the market's, in standard-deviation units. It drifted toward quiet, low-volatility stocks near their 52-week highs and away from lottery-like spikers. These are textbook anomalies: the low-volatility effect, the MAX effect and the 52-week-high effect.

Limits and disclosures

  • Peek during debugging: an early version of the picker was run once on Vietnam's 2024–26 data before the design was final. The only change afterwards was letting validation choose a slower trading pace, which validation costs already justified. Vietnam's picker still lost.
  • Bug fix: the shock-rebound benchmark first averaged log returns, which flattered it slightly. It was corrected and everything was rerun. The conclusions didn't change.
  • Survivorship: stocks were sampled from today's listings, so companies that went bust before today are missing. That flatters the markets and shock-buying. It affects the fly and the random pickers equally.
  • Costs use today's spreads for the whole history. Nordic and UK small caps often raise money through deeply discounted share issues that free price data records as crashes, so those two markets are noisier.
  • "Tokyo small caps", "First North" and "AIM" are approximated by market-cap cut-offs, because the data source doesn't label exchange segments.
  • Settings were chosen across all four markets together, from 4 picker and 6 rebound options. Every option lost money on validation.
